# Functional gene annotation: an overview

[中文](field-overview.zh.md) · [Knowledge units](README.md)

## 1. What it is

Functional gene annotation asks what the protein and RNA products of a genome do: which molecular functions they carry out, in which biological processes, in which parts of the cell, and in which pathways. It starts from a released protein set (`proteins.faa`) produced by [gene-structure-annotation](https://github.com/Xuzhen-Li/gene-structure-annotation) (claim 1). Most knowledge about gene function comes from experiments in a relatively small number of model organisms (claim 2), so labels for a new genome are mostly *inferred*: transferred from similar sequences or from orthologs (claim 3). The evidence code attached to a label says how it was obtained (claim 15).

## 2. The questions and the concepts behind them

**What can be inferred from sequence similarity and evolutionary history?** Orthologs and paralogs are two types of homologous genes: *orthologs* evolved by vertical descent from a single ancestral gene, *paralogs* by duplication, and telling the two apart is critical for reliable functional annotation of newly sequenced genomes (claim 4). Orthology assignment is well suited to functional inference, but it is computationally intensive at large scale, so the less precise transfer of function by homology is still the default for genome annotation (claim 3). Similarity-based tools such as Blast2GO and PANNZER2 assign GO terms from homology searches (claim 5). eggNOG-mapper annotates from precomputed orthology assignments (claim 6), and OrthoFinder infers orthogroups and orthologs across genomes (claim 7). Book: chapters 2, 5 and 8.

**Which parts and structures does a protein contain?** Proteins are classified into families and domains by resources such as InterPro, which integrates signatures from several member databases, and Pfam (claim 8). Structure search tools such as Foldseek align a query structure against a structure database (claim 9). Localisation predictors such as TargetP 2.0 identify N-terminal sorting signals that direct proteins to the secretory pathway, mitochondria and chloroplasts or other plastids (claim 10). Book: chapters 4, 9 and 14.

**How are functions written in standard vocabularies and pathways?** Free-text names are hard to compute with, so functions are mapped to controlled systems. The *Gene Ontology* (GO) describes biological processes, molecular functions and cellular components in structured vocabularies (claims 11 and 12); GO *annotations* are evidence-supported statements about a gene product (claim 11). *Enzyme Commission* (EC) numbers are four-element codes from the IUBMB enzyme classification (claim 13). The *KEGG Orthology* (KO) links genes and proteins to pathway maps (claim 14). *Evidence codes* record where a label comes from; the code IEA marks annotations that cannot generally be traced to an experimental source (claim 15). Book: chapters 3, 5 and 7.

**What needs special attention in plants and beyond protein-coding genes?** Many plant immune receptors (NLRs) are highly diversified within one genome and between individuals of a species (claim 16); plant gene families have their own chapter in the book. GO also covers non-coding RNAs (claim 11). Co-expression networks give an initial way to explore functional associations from expression data, with caveats such as annotation propagation and guilt-by-association (claims 17 and 18). Book: chapters 10 to 12.

**How far can a function table be trusted, and how is it used?** Labels from automated methods are not experimental results: IEA annotations cannot generally be traced to an experimental source (claim 15). Overlooked aspects of GO can cause erroneous results (claim 12), and errors can spread when a fragmented proteome is used as a reference, as OMArk found for avian annotation (claim 19). Enrichment analysis relates gene lists to ontologies and pathways, and its authors stress the need for up-to-date annotation databases (claim 20). Thresholds and gates are written only in [`docs/EVALUATION.md`](../EVALUATION.md). Book: chapters 15 and 19.
